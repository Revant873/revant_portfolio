Sheet 02.2 · datum A · additive manufacturing

VDI university prize · 2026 cycle

The optimiser said 98.29%. The bridges collapsed anyway.

Fraunhofer IPT runs a 3D printer with no belts, no pulleys and no lead screws. Motion in X and Y comes from a Beckhoff XPlanar — permanent-magnet movers levitating over magnetic tiles, contactless, clean-room capable. Nobody had characterised it for polycaprolactone implant scaffolds. This is the parameter set I established, and the reason I didn't ship the one the software recommended.

Method

  • Screened every factor that could touch print quality across six categories with an Ishikawa diagram, then reduced to three worth spending runs on: layer height, raster width, and mover levitation height.
  • Fixed the baseline first with a 3³ full-factorial pilot on a conventional MEX printer — 170 °C, 15 mm/s, 0.4 mm nozzle.
  • Ran a 19-run Box–Behnken design with six replicated centre points on the magnetic planar drive machine.
  • Measured nine points per specimen with 0.01 mm vernier calipers — two length, two width, five pore — reduced by a Python script that folds in caliper uncertainty and 1.5% shrinkage compensation.

The confrontation

Stat-Ease returned a numerical optimum and predicted 98.29% dimensional accuracy from it. Printed, its bridges collapsed. The set I'd chosen from watching parts come off the bed predicted less — 96.50% — and produced structurally sound scaffolds.

The uncomfortable part is the middle row of the table. The software's set actually measured more accurate — 97.47% against my 97.14%. On the only metric the model was optimising, it won. It was still the wrong answer, because dimensional accuracy doesn't capture whether an unsupported span survives.

What the statistics said

  • Layer height dominates: p < 0.0001 at F = 131.1, with a significant quadratic term (F = 54.3). Model R² = 0.949, lack-of-fit non-significant at p = 0.39.
  • Raster width (p = 0.82) and mover levitation height (p = 0.67) were not significant — a useful negative result, because it means the XPlanar's levitation freedom costs nothing in dimensional accuracy anywhere in the 1–4 mm band.
  • At 0.1 mm layer height accuracy fell to roughly 73% and several runs didn't survive printing at all. 0.2 mm averaged 95.01%; 0.3 mm averaged 95.51% but carried a consistently over-extruded first layer.

Where the machine stops

The lower bound of the whole study came from hitting a physical wall. At a 0.5 mm levitation gap the system pushed past 60 °C and threw errors: concentrated magnetic flux drives eddy currents into nearby conductors, the narrow gap starves convective cooling, and the field destabilises sensor feedback. Raising the gap to 1 mm fixed it. No amount of parameter tuning routes around Joule heating — you move the gap.

I also recommended replacing the granulate extruder rather than tuning around it. Heat loss made extrusion inconsistent, and motor vibration was dropping screws into the barrel chamber.
