Sheet 02.5 · locomotion

Build. from a published open design Paper. published, IJSR, Nov 2025

What a battery does to a small biped's gait.

I built a published biped design as a known-good platform for learning inverse kinematics. The next step was to replace the bench supply with a battery. That simple change turned into a study of mass placement, swing inertia, and the mechanical limits that a controller cannot remove.

My rolePlatform builder and author of the follow-on paper
EvidenceKnown-good open design, gait experiments, and a scaling analysis
Key pointBattery position changes balance targets and can roughly double hip swing inertia

The mechanical design is not mine. I built the Arduino Controlled Robotic Biped published by Technovation in 2020. I followed the printed-part, hobby-servo, and Arduino design as published, then used the working platform for the battery-placement study and the paper in Part B.

The Build

Ongoing

Why build someone else's robot

Inverse kinematics is easy to read about and hard to feel. Working from a design that already walked meant the failures I hit were my failures of understanding rather than failures of the machine. when the foot landed wrong, the geometry was fine and my solution wasn't.

That's the whole reason to build a known-good platform first. It removes one variable, and the variable it removes is the one you're least equipped to debug.

What the build revealed

It runs off a bench supply. That's a reasonable choice for a platform meant to be experimented on. you never lose a run to a flat pack, and the mass budget stays clean. But it means the robot has never carried its own power, and I wanted it to.

A battery isn't an accessory. It's roughly two-thirds the mass of a leg segment, and it has to be somewhere.

Strictly, the inverse kinematics doesn't change. The links are the same length, so the mapping from foot position to joint angles is the same mapping. What changes is what you ask it for: move the centre of mass and the foot placements that keep the robot balanced move with it, so the trajectory the solver is asked to hit is a different trajectory. The maths is untouched; the targets aren't.

Where the pack goes

Which turns the battery into a mass-placement decision rather than a wiring one, and that decision is governed by a quantity the paper in Part B names directly: how much of the leg's mass sits below the knee, and therefore how much inertia the hip has to swing.

The figure below is that trade-off computed from stated assumptions. Sliding a 180-gram pack from the pelvis to mid-shank roughly doubles the swing inertia about the hip. same robot, same links, same controller, twice the work per step.

Part B

The Paper

Published · two authors

The argument

Mass distribution changes the gait problem before the controller touches it. Legged robots usually fail at contact, and the literature usually answers with better control. The paper argues the constraint is mechanical before it is algorithmic: what matters at touchdown is which side of the joint is in charge, the limb or the drivetrain.

This scaling argument depends on the paper's geometric-similarity and actuator-scaling assumptions. Under those assumptions, segment mass grows as s³ while limb inertia grows as s⁵. The inertial torque needed for the same swing profile therefore grows faster than continuous actuator torque, which the paper models as scaling with s³. As the robot gets larger, the mechanical demand pulls away from what the motor can sustain within that model.

Two doctrines

  • Underactuation by design. not removing actuators, but building stance–swing asymmetry into hardware. Clutches that lock under load and release in swing, rising-rate elastics that are soft at touchdown and authoritative at toe-off, couplings that share load in stance and disengage for clearance. Behaviours that become probable without the controller having to insist.
  • Design-to-inertia. treating moments of inertia, reflected inertia through gearing, and mass placement as budgeted design variables. Keep distal mass low, keep gear ratios moderate enough that joints stay backdrivable, and size thermal paths for continuous walking rather than for isolated peak events.

Four metrics

The paper's practical contribution is a small set of plant-level numbers that can be computed from CAD and basic tests, so mechanical choices become as comparable across platforms as controller performance already is.

Plant-level metrics proposed for cross-platform comparison
MetricDefinitionWhat it exposes
RIR(n²Jm + Jg) / JlinkReflected inertia ratio. whether the limb or the drivetrain dominates the joint. Small means backdrivable.
DMF(mshank + mfoot) / mlegDistal mass fraction. how much mass the leg has to swing and arrest at every step.
RAI1 − mmotors,distal / mlegRemote actuation index. how completely actuators have been moved off the moving segments.
ERBpower saved vs baselineEnergy-recycling benefit. whether clutched or parallel elastics actually cut electrical burden.

What the metrics say about my own platform

Badly, and that's the honest reading. Hobby servos are high-ratio and effectively non-backdrivable, so RIR is large at every joint. the drivetrain dominates, not the limb. The servos are joint-mounted rather than routed from the pelvis, so RAI is near zero and DMF is high before a battery is added at all.

None of which is a criticism of the original design. It's a low-cost platform built to survive gait experiments, and it does that well. But it sits at the opposite end of every axis the paper argues for, which is a useful place to be standing when you're trying to understand why the argument matters.
