Build. ongoing Paper. published, IJSR, Sept 2025

A tendon-driven hand designed around repair.

This page covers two connected pieces of work: a tendon-driven hand that is still in development, and a published framework for measuring how hard tendon-driven hands are to repair. Repeated rebuilds made service access, tensioning, and repair time part of the design brief.

The Build

Ongoing

What it is

A printed anthropomorphic hand driven by tendons rather than a motor at each joint. Cable runs from the fingertip, down the back of each phalanx, through guides, to an anchor in the palm. Pull the cable and the finger curls; the path the cable takes is what decides how it curls.

The current build has one fully assembled and actuated finger. The remaining digits are mounted stubs. I am keeping that status visible because the maintainability work comes from the real effort of rebuilding this prototype.

Why it keeps changing

The recurring failures are ordinary: frayed tendons, slipped knots, and tension drift after repeated cycles. Each one can cost an afternoon because reaching the anchor requires partial disassembly.

Each rebuild now has a specific service goal, such as moving the anchor, reducing the fastener count, or making tension adjustment repeatable.

So the changes are rarely about dexterity. They're about where the anchor sits, how many fasteners stand between me and it, whether the tension can be dialled back in without guessing. That's a serviceability problem, and I only started calling it that after the fourth or fifth time.

The Paper

Published · sole author

The gap

Every tendon-driven hand in the literature reports degrees of freedom, grip force, workspace, assembly time. The LEAP Hand leads on cost and build speed. The SoftHand-A on underactuation and tactile sensing. The ORCA Hand fits ratcheting spools and poppable joints specifically to make maintenance easier. and still publishes no repair-time number.

Aerospace and automotive have treated Mean Time To Repair as a first-class specification for decades. Robotic hands, the machines whose defining component is a cable that wears out, mostly don't measure it at all.

Six indicators

The paper adapts MTTR into six measures specific to tendon hands, each mapped onto a stage of the actual repair sequence. diagnose, access, repair, reassemble, verify.

Serviceability KPIs for tendon-driven hands
IndicatorUnitWhat it captures
Re-string timeRST · minFull cycle to replace one tendon and restore motion. Proposed lab baseline: ≤ 10 min per finger.
Tool changesTC · countDistinct tools a repair needs. Target: no more than two standard tools.
Fastener countFC · countScrews removed and reinstalled. Captive screws and split covers drive this down.
Anchor accessibilityAA · yes/noWhether the tendon anchor can be reached without major disassembly.
Tensioning resolutionTR · mm/turnHow finely tension can be set. Coarse resolution means guessing.
Spare readinessSR · yes/noPre-made spare, or cut, crimp and knot on the spot.

The benchmark

Sever or release a tendon deliberately. Restore the finger to full range. Start the clock when the repairer picks up the first tool, stop it when the finger completes a defined flexion–extension sequence. Three trials per hand, at least two different repairers, report the median so one expert's muscle memory doesn't set the number.

No force sensors, no motion capture, no custom jigs. hand tools and a stopwatch, so any lab can run it. Results go on a Maintainability Card alongside degrees of freedom and grip force, not instead of them.
