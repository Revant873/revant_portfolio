Sheet 02.3 · datum A · liquid handling

Delivered · mechanical design lead · four-person team

From 11 requirements to a production-ready assembly plan.

In a team of four at Hochschule Rhein-Waal, I led the mechanical design of an automatic cocktail maker. We moved from a written requirements list to CAD, risk analysis, costing, manufacturing decisions, and a three-station assembly plan. The machine had to dispense from seven bottles, produce six layered drinks, and clean every wetted path without disassembly.

Key pointThe risk analysis changed the tank, electrical enclosure, and cleaning design

My role. mechanical design lead. I wrote the eleven-item requirements list, drew the initial concept sketch and the executive summary, and owned the mechanical design through to the production plan: CAD model, 3D assembly, technical drawing set, mechanical DFMEA, bill of materials, make-or-buy decisions and assembly layout. Hasan Tasawar Kiani worked with me across the CAD and documentation. Praneel Bhatia and Faraz Mehmood Sheikh led the electrical architecture and component selection. Four-person team, Hochschule Rhein-Waal, WT 2023/24.

The requirements came first

Nothing was modelled until the list existed. Eleven requirements, written before any geometry, and the fourth one is the reason this project keeps showing up in everything I've done since.

  1. Hold and dispense from at least seven bottles simultaneously
  2. Craft a minimum of six layered cocktails without a mixing stage
  3. Clean every wetted path automatically
  4. Modularity and repairability. straightforward repairs and part replacement
  5. Sustainable materials
  6. Aesthetics, ergonomics, stability, usability, durability, environmental responsibility, energy efficiency

Requirement four focused on cleaning and service access. I later used the same maintainability questions in my work on tendon-driven robotic hands: what fails, how quickly can it be reached, and what has to come apart first?

What got built

  • A 700 × 500 × 457 mm envelope at 18 kg, with eight dispensing stations across the upper deck.
  • A servo-actuated access panel opening through 160° for bottle loading and service.
  • A partitioned electrical box, separated from the wetted volume for thermal and liquid isolation.
  • A common tank and cleaning circuit with a fixed cleaning ball and solenoid valves, so alcohol paths flush without teardown.
  • A full two-sheet drawing set at 1:5 to ISO 2768-mK, with surface and edge finishes specified.

Mechanical design

Material choice followed function rather than convenience. Anything holding liquid is PP homopolymer. the 4.42 kg common tank and the electrical box. because it is chemically stable against spirits and moulds cleanly. Everything structural is 6061 aluminium alloy.

Every make-part carried a process and a parting line before it reached the BOM. The main body is cold-chamber die-cast then CNC-milled; the grill panels and LCD panel are die-cast; the flat panels, partitions and maintenance door are laser-cut, with the lower partition bent first; and the eight servomotor handles are bent then finished by sinker EDM.

The bill of materials exposed an unexpected cost driver. Eight 1.3 gram servomotor handles cost €34.18 in total, compared with €18.68 for the main body, because each handle required €4.27 of sinker EDM work. The smallest parts, not the chassis, were the first place to reduce cost.

What the DFMEA changed

We scored the mechanical system by risk priority number. severity × occurrence × detection. and the common tank came back worst on both counts.

The tank leak scored severity 10. the only 10 in the analysis. because liquid reaching the control electronics is a fire risk, not an inconvenience. That single row is why the electrical box is a physically partitioned volume rather than a bracket inside the chassis, why overflow sensors are specified, and why the nozzle is designed to self-clean after every dispense. The analysis came before the geometry, and it changed the geometry.
